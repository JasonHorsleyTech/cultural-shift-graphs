## Build breakdown ($ millions)

Restaurant economics are dominated by physical operations: ingredients, line cooks, the building they cook in, and the utilities/repairs/equipment that keep service running. Chipotle in FY2024 ran 3,726 restaurants (corporate-owned, ~100% of the system) and produced ~$3.0M of average unit volume per restaurant — a productivity number that's roughly double McDonald's franchise-store AUV and is the engine of Chipotle's industry-leading restaurant-level operating margin (26.7%). The build stack below is essentially the cost of running 3,700+ kitchens, every line item of which is required to produce a burrito.

Per the ticket guidance, **occupancy is classified as build, not sell** — for a restaurant the storefront IS the factory. This is the central methodology call for this ticket and the choice is consistent with the cross-product rubric (a Whirlpool factory's rent is build; a Chipotle restaurant's rent is the same kind of cost — the place where the product is physically made).

- Food, beverage and packaging — chicken, beef (steak, barbacoa, brisket LTO), pork (carnitas), sofritas (tofu), avocados, dairy (cheese, sour cream, queso blanco), produce (onions, tomatoes, cilantro, lettuce, peppers), rice, beans, tortillas, drinks, foil/burrito wrappers, bowls, lids, napkins. FY2024 mix shifted toward higher-cost proteins (Smoked Brisket LTO, Braised Beef Barbacoa initiative) and avocado inflation pushed this line up 30bp YoY: 3375
- Labor — restaurant crew (line cooks, food prep, cashiers, expediters, delivery handoff), shift leaders, kitchen managers, restaurant general managers, restaurateurs, plus restaurant-level training. Includes the FY2024 California fast-food $20/hr minimum wage impact (AB 1228 / FAST Act, effective April 2024). All restaurant labor is build under our rubric — without it there is no burrito: 2790
- Occupancy — rent on 3,726 restaurant locations (the storefront IS the factory; counted as build per ticket methodology), common-area maintenance, real estate taxes, lease-incentive amortization. Chipotle leases nearly all locations; very few are owned outright: 563
- Other operating ex-marketing — delivery service fees paid to DoorDash and Uber Eats marketplaces (Chipotle digital sales were 35.0% of food and beverage sales in Q4 2024, of which marketplace delivery is a meaningful share), credit card and payment processing fees, utilities (electric, gas, water, waste), restaurant supplies and smallware, repairs and maintenance, restaurant-level technology (Chipotlane order screens, kitchen display systems, drive-thru audio, POS), insurance, restaurant manager training and the biennial All Managers Conference, recruiting, restaurant-level legal/compliance — but EXCLUDING marketing/advertising/promo which is broken out into Sell: 1259
- Depreciation and amortization — restaurant build-outs (kitchen equipment, drive-thru/Chipotlane infrastructure, dining room buildouts, signage), kitchen technology (Hyphen / makeline robotics being piloted, kitchen display systems), capitalized internal-use software for the app/web ordering platform: 335
- Pre-opening costs — labor and supply costs incurred before a new restaurant opens (roughly 304 net new openings in FY2024, plus restaurant remodels), training crews ahead of opening day: 42

**Build total: 8365**

## Sell breakdown ($ millions)

Chipotle is famously underweight on traditional advertising relative to QSR peers. The brand has historically grown through word-of-mouth, in-store experience, social/influencer earned media (TikTok, Instagram), the Chipotle Rewards loyalty program (~40 million members), and selective high-impact campaigns (the "Adan Cantor" Braised Beef Barbacoa launch, Brisket LTO, college-night promos, free guac days). Marketing as a percent of revenue has run in the 2.5-3.3% range for the past seven years — well below McDonald's (~3.5-4%), Yum brands (~4-5% of system sales applied for advertising co-ops), and Wendy's. FY2024 saw a modest step-up versus FY2023 driven by the Braised Beef Barbacoa marketing initiative and Brisket LTO support, but the absolute spend still represents a low share of total cost.

- Advertising / measured media + creative + digital + brand activation — TV (light, targeted to LTO launches), digital video, paid social, paid search, retail/loyalty marketing, Chipotle Rewards member campaigns, sponsorships (NCAA partnerships, college esports), influencer programs, in-app promotions: 240
- Sales force compensation — minimal in restaurant context; allocation here covers the corporate marketing team headcount, brand strategy, creative agency retainer (Venables Bell & Partners historically, plus rotating digital partners), media-buying agency: 25
- Trade promotion / loyalty incentives / promo discounts — Chipotle Rewards program redemption costs (free entrees, BOGO offers), promotional discounting (free delivery promos, value menu testing), digital ordering incentives, college-night and back-to-school promos, athlete/team partnerships: 45

**Sell total: 310**

## Methodology notes

**The occupancy-as-build call is the central classification choice.** The cross-product rubric treats "retail real estate (storefronts, not factories)" as sell — but for a fast-casual restaurant the storefront IS the factory. Food is prepped, cooked, assembled, and handed to the customer in the same physical box, often in front of the customer's eyes. The "factory" exists only because the customer-facing servery exists; you cannot separate them. Classified Chipotle's $563M of occupancy as build, consistent with how factory rent is treated for a manufacturer. If reclassified as sell (the alternate reading where the restaurant location is partly retail real estate the way a Macy's storefront is), build would drop to $7,802M and sell would rise to $873M, yielding build_pct of 89.9% — still extremely build-heavy but materially less so. The chosen 96.4% reflects the production-facility framing; flagged for the reader.

**Delivery service fees are build, not sell.** Chipotle paid significant fees to DoorDash and Uber Eats marketplaces in FY2024 (digital sales were 35% of Q4 food/bev sales, of which marketplace delivery is roughly a third; the remaining two-thirds are first-party order-ahead via the Chipotle app and web for in-store pickup or Chipotlane drive-thru). Marketplace fees are arguably channel acquisition (a sell-flavored cost) but functionally they are distribution — the cost of physically getting a burrito from the kitchen to a customer who chose not to come in. Per the cross-product rubric where distribution/logistics is build, delivery fees stayed in build (within the $1,259M "other operating ex-marketing" bucket). If reclassified as sell, build_pct would shift down by ~2-3pp.

**Marketing/promo is the only true sell line in this taxonomy.** Chipotle's 10-K folds marketing and promotional expense into "Other operating costs" rather than breaking it out as a separate income-statement line (unlike Clorox, P&G, etc., which disclose a separate advertising line per FASB requirements). FY2023 marketing was reported in narrative form at approximately $264M (~2.7% of revenue per Statista's tracking and earnings-call references). FY2024 narrative explicitly noted "higher advertising and marketing promotions expense" supporting the Braised Beef Barbacoa initiative and Brisket LTO. Estimated FY2024 marketing/promo at $310M (~2.7-2.8% of revenue), within the 2.5-3.3% historical band. The methodology stress-test: at the high end (3.3% = $373M), build_pct would be 95.7%; at the low end (2.5% = $283M), build_pct would be 96.7% — answer is robust to ±0.5pp on this assumption.

**Why the Chipotle ratio is the highest on the consumer panel (or close to it).** Three structural reasons drive build_pct toward the asymptote:

1. **The product is physical work.** Every burrito is hand-assembled by a person in real time. There is no factory automation, no third-party manufacturing, no licensing model that would shift work off the company's books. Labor (24.7% of revenue) is a quarter of the cost stack on its own — far above any consumer-goods category on the panel.
2. **Chipotle's marketing model is structurally light.** The brand was built (and rebuilt after the 2015-2016 food safety crisis) on word-of-mouth, social, in-store experience, and selective LTOs. The company has explicitly chosen not to compete on TV ad volume against McDonald's or Wendy's — they cannot win that arms race and don't try. Marketing/promo at 2.7% of revenue is below the fast-casual industry average (~3-5%) and far below QSR-with-franchise-co-op spending.
3. **The corporate-owned model captures all the operations dollars.** Unlike McDonald's where 95% of revenue comes from franchise royalties + lease income + initial fees (so the parent's books reflect a real-estate-and-licensing economics, not burger production), Chipotle's books reflect the actual cost of producing 3.7 million burritos a day. Every food, labor, and occupancy dollar lands on the parent income statement.

**Comparison to McDonald's (the dropped ticket).** This is exactly why McDonald's was excluded. McDonald's FY2024 reported revenue ~$25.9B, but ~95% of restaurants are franchised — so consolidated revenue is dominated by royalties and rent collected from franchisees, not the food cost of a Big Mac. Building a "Big Mac build vs. sell" from McDonald's parent books would measure McDonald's the licensor, not the burger. Chipotle gives us the unit-economic answer the chart is asking for, and the resulting ratio (96.4% build) is the structural truth about restaurant production: the work of making and serving food is overwhelmingly physical, and only a thin sliver is brand-building expenditure.

**Why operating margin (16.9%) is low despite the high build ratio.** Build-heaviness does not imply low profit; it implies the cost stack is operations-dominated. Restaurants are inherently operations-intensive but the brand premium and pricing power (Chipotle's average check has risen materially since 2020 through a combination of menu price and protein mix) deliver healthy operating margin. The 16.9% operating margin is unusually high for the fast-casual industry (sector average ~6-12%), and the 26.7% restaurant-level operating margin is best-in-class.

**Restaurant-level operating margin vs. operating margin.** Chipotle reports restaurant-level operating margin (26.7%) as a key non-GAAP metric — this excludes G&A, D&A, pre-opening, and impairment, treating restaurants as a unit-of-production. Our taxonomy captures this implicitly: Build + Sell ≈ direct restaurant operating costs + restaurant-allocated marketing + restaurant D&A + restaurant pre-opening. Operating margin (16.9%) is what's left after subtracting corporate G&A (treated here as overhead). Both numbers are visible in the reconciliation and consistent with the chart's framework.

**Confidence: high.** Single-segment US-listed company with detailed income-statement disclosure, restaurant-level economics broken out as a non-GAAP metric, MD&A discussion of every cost line, and an unusually clean parent-vs-product alignment (no franchise distortion, no segment allocation issues, no material non-restaurant business). The only methodological judgment calls are (a) classifying occupancy as build (justified for restaurant context, flagged) and (b) estimating marketing/promo from narrative since it isn't broken out as a separate line. Both choices are defensible and the headline ratio is robust to ±2pp under alternate classifications.

**What's surprising in the numbers.** Three things worth noting honestly:

- **The marketing line is shockingly small in absolute dollars.** Chipotle's $310M of estimated marketing/promo is less than what Coca-Cola spent on a single global brand campaign and less than what Apple spent on iPhone launch marketing in a single quarter. A brand this culturally visible is built on operational excellence and earned media, not paid media.
- **Labor is 9× larger than marketing.** This is the central truth of restaurant economics — the people in the box are the brand, the product, and the cost. Inverting that ratio (more marketing, less labor) is what every QSR franchise model attempts; Chipotle has explicitly not gone that direction.
- **The high build_pct does not crowd out profit.** A 96% build ratio coexisting with a 16.9% operating margin is the chart's interesting finding: operations-intensity and profitability are not opposites. Pricing power and operational leverage (the +14.6% revenue growth rolling through largely fixed restaurant infrastructure) deliver margin even when the cost stack looks "industrial."
