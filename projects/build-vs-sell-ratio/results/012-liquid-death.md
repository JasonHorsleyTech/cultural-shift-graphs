## Build breakdown ($ millions)

Liquid Death's manufacturing model is fully outsourced: still mountain water is sourced from a spring in the Austrian Alps and shipped trans-Atlantic in finished cans (or in bulk to a US co-packing partner), while sparkling waters and flavored "Severed Limes," "Mango Chainsaw," "Berry It Alive," "Convicted Melon," etc. are produced through US-based co-pack arrangements. There is no owned manufacturing plant or owned distribution fleet. The "build" cost stack is therefore very thin on internal labor and capex, and very heavy on contracted unit costs (aluminum cans, co-pack fees, freight) that show up as cost of goods sold on the brand owner's books.

- COGS — water (Austrian spring license + sparkling US sources), aluminum cans (12oz and 16.9oz tallboy formats), co-pack manufacturing fees, primary packaging (label printing, multi-packs), inbound freight including trans-Atlantic ocean freight for Austrian product: 110
- Distribution / outbound logistics (warehousing across US distribution centers, freight to retailer DCs, last-mile to convenience-store DSD where applicable): 25
- R&D / product development (flavor extension lineup, line of iced teas launched 2024, packaging design): 3
- Customer support (DTC site customer service, retail-partner relationship management — small): 2

**Build total: 140**

## Sell breakdown ($ millions)

This is the heart of Liquid Death's cost structure and the entire reason the brand is interesting as a "sell-heavy" data point on the chart. Founder Mike Cessario has been consistent in interviews that marketing investment runs roughly a third of revenue — substantially above CPG-beverage norms (Coca-Cola ~9%, Monster ~10%, Celsius ~17-20%) and above even other premium-water peers (Fiji, Voss). The brand is also unusual in running an in-house creative studio that produces viral content (the Tony Hawk blood-skateboard, the Martha Stewart cease-and-desist parody, the "Greatest Hates" album of customer complaints, the Steve-O collab, etc.) — meaning agency fees are partly internalized as headcount in the marketing line.

- Advertising / measured media + creative production + influencer & celebrity partnerships + viral stunt budget + paid social + podcast and YouTube sponsorships + in-house creative studio payroll (estimated at ~30% of revenue per founder commentary): 100
- Brand activations + sponsorships (Live Nation venue exclusivity payments and activations, music festival presence — Riot Fest, Bonnaroo, etc., MMA/UFC and NHL sponsorships, Vans skateboarding events): 18
- Sales force compensation (national-account sales reps for Walmart, Target, 7-Eleven, Costco, Whole Foods, Amazon — relatively small headcount given concentrated channel): 12
- Trade promotion / slotting / dealer incentives (slotting fees for new market and new SKU expansion, especially heavy in 2024 with iced tea launch and convenience-store push, distributor margin support, end-cap and cooler-door programs): 20

**Sell total: 150**

## Excluded overhead ($ millions)

- Corporate G&A — executive comp, HQ (Los Angeles), HR, IT, finance & accounting payroll: 22
- Finance, legal, IR-equivalent (private-company investor relations, FP&A, treasury, IP/trademark legal which is non-trivial given the brand's litigation/parody posture): 8
- Other overhead — board fees, professional services, software subscriptions, insurance: 5

**Overhead total: 35**

## Methodology notes

**Revenue scale — triangulation, not disclosure.** Liquid Death is private and does not publish financial statements. The available signals are: (a) Forbes/CNBC reporting that 2023 revenue was approximately $263M; (b) the prior data point of ~$130M for FY2022 (cited in 2023 funding-round coverage); (c) industry trade press indicating continued strong YoY growth into 2024 driven by the iced tea launch (April 2024), the Costco rollout, expanded Live Nation venue presence, and convenience-store expansion. Annual revenue growth ran roughly 100% in 2022→2023 ($130M → $263M). A more conservative 25-30% growth rate for 2023→2024 (consistent with peer Celsius decelerating from hyper-growth as scale increases, and reports of moderating canned-water category growth) puts FY2024 in the **$320-345M range**. Used **$330M** as the midpoint estimate. This is the single largest uncertainty in the analysis — if true 2024 revenue were $260M (no growth) or $400M (continued doubling), build_pct would shift modestly because cost ratios scale with revenue but fixed costs (G&A, sales-force) don't.

**Confidence is low — explicitly so.** Per plan.md's confidence rubric, "low" applies to "private company...where the SKU economics are obscured by the parent...best-effort with disclosed limitations." Liquid Death is the canonical "private DTC startup" case: no audited financials, no required disclosures, no segment reporting (because it's single-segment but also nothing to disclose). All four primary buckets are estimates derived from (a) founder interview commentary on marketing intensity, (b) public peer cost structures (Celsius, Monster, National Beverage), and (c) industry-typical co-pack and aluminum-can unit economics. The directional answer — "this is a sell-heavy outlier vs. CPG beverages" — is robust; the precise build_pct is not.

**Marketing intensity — the sell-side anchor.** Mike Cessario has said publicly on multiple podcast appearances (My First Million, Marketing Examined newsletter interviews, How I Built This NPR appearance) that marketing runs "roughly a third of revenue" and that Liquid Death deliberately under-invests in profit relative to brand-building. At ~30% of revenue, FY24 marketing is ~$100M. Adding sponsorships ($18M), sales force ($12M), and trade promotion ($20M) gets the total sell number to $150M (~45% of revenue). For comparison: Coca-Cola's total sell line (per ticket 005) was 13.0% of revenue; Bud Light's ~12%; Monster ~10-11%; even Celsius (the closest "growth-mode beverage" peer) runs total sell intensity around 22-24%. Liquid Death's ~45% sell intensity is the structural defining feature of the brand and the chart point.

**COGS — co-packed beverage benchmarks.** With no owned manufacturing, COGS for Liquid Death is essentially: (aluminum can cost) + (co-pack fee) + (water sourcing/license) + (freight). Aluminum can cost in 2024 ran ~$0.10-0.15 per 12oz can after the 2022 aluminum-cost spike normalized; 16.9oz tallboys ~$0.12-0.18; co-pack fees typically $0.05-0.10/can; freight from Austria for still water adds ~$0.05-0.10/can; secondary packaging for 8/12-packs adds modest cost. Total cost-per-can in the bottom-of-bag retail price ($1.50-2.00 retail / $0.90-1.30 wholesale) suggests COGS-to-revenue around 32-40% on the wholesale base. Adding outbound freight (which is in COGS for most beverage peers), total cost-of-sales runs ~40-45% of revenue. Used **$110M COGS + $25M distribution = $135M cost-of-sales (40.9% of revenue)**, which is at the lower end of the canned-water peer range — Liquid Death's positioning as "premium tallboy" allows higher per-unit pricing than National Beverage's LaCroix (which runs ~62% COGS) and pulls cost ratios down.

**Profitability — likely near breakeven, possibly modest loss.** Cessario has publicly said Liquid Death "could be more profitable but is choosing not to be" — a posture consistent with venture-backed growth-stage company priorities. Public reporting around the March 2024 Series F at $1.4B valuation did not disclose profitability, but it implied the company was not yet profitable and was using fresh capital to fund continued growth and channel expansion. Used **$5M operating profit** as the residual plug — essentially breakeven within the precision of the estimates. A reasonable range is **-$15M to +$25M** depending on (a) actual marketing spend (could be 28-35% of revenue), (b) actual COGS (could be 38-45%), and (c) one-time costs in the 2024 iced tea launch. The ratio is robust to this range — sell intensity dominates.

**Sensitivity check.** Three plausible alternate scenarios:
- **More sell-heavy** (marketing 35%, COGS 38%): build = $130M, sell = $165M, build_pct = **44.1%**
- **As classified** (marketing 30%, COGS 41%): build = $140M, sell = $150M, build_pct = **48.3%** (headline)
- **Less sell-heavy** (marketing 25%, COGS 45%): build = $150M, sell = $130M, build_pct = **53.6%**

All three land in the 44-54% range — squarely inside the ticket's 35-55% prediction band, and consistently 25+ percentage points more sell-heavy than the CPG-beverage cluster (Coca-Cola 67%, Bud Light 82%, Jack Daniel's 64%). The directional answer is robust even to wide swings in the assumptions.

**Why no R&D worth speaking of.** Beverages — even premium beverages — have very low R&D as a separate line. Flavor development, packaging design, and new-SKU formulation are typically done by the co-pack partner or by small in-house brand teams that get coded into marketing/brand budget. Used a $3M token figure for R&D recognizing that any "innovation" line at Liquid Death is partly inside marketing payroll (creative directors who also drive product line extensions) and partly inside COGS (co-pack development fees rolled into per-can pricing).

**Trans-Atlantic freight is real but doesn't dominate.** Liquid Death's still water comes from Austria. Ocean container freight (40-foot reefer not required for water; standard dry container) runs $2,000-4,000 per container in normal conditions, holding ~25,000 cans = $0.08-0.16/can. Sparkling waters and flavored variants are produced domestically (per founder commentary on operational scaling — ocean freight for carbonated product is impractical). Estimated mix: ~50% of volume is Austrian-sourced still, ~50% US-domestic sparkling/flavored. Effective freight burden ~$0.05/can blended, included inside COGS.

**Sales force is small — concentrated retail channel.** Unlike Coca-Cola or Anheuser-Busch which run thousands of route-sales reps, Liquid Death sells through ~6-8 major retail partners (Walmart, Target, 7-Eleven, Whole Foods, Costco, Amazon, plus Live Nation and DTC) with concentrated national-account selling. Estimated sales-force headcount of ~30-50 people fully loaded ≈ $10-15M annual cost. This is materially smaller than legacy beverage peers' sell-side payroll burden, but the ratio is offset by higher trade-promotion and slotting costs typical of a brand expanding shelf space against entrenched incumbents.

**Slotting and trade promotion.** New-SKU launches at major retailers carry slotting fees (one-time per-SKU-per-store payments to secure shelf placement) and ongoing trade promotion (ad allowances, end-cap programs, cooler-door space, distributor margin support). Liquid Death's 2024 expansion into iced tea (4 SKUs), continued sparkling-water flavor extensions, and convenience-store push all incurred meaningful slotting. Estimated $20M for FY24 — high relative to mature peers, normal for a brand at this growth stage. Per CPG convention, some of this is netted against revenue rather than appearing as expense, so the true gross figure may be 1.5-2× this and the corresponding "gross revenue" higher; the build_pct doesn't move because both numerator and denominator shift proportionally.

**Live Nation partnership — both customer and distribution channel.** Live Nation invested in Liquid Death and signed a long-term partnership making Liquid Death the exclusive on-tap canned water at Live Nation venues. This is unusual structurally — Live Nation is simultaneously an investor, a customer (buying Liquid Death for its venues), and a distribution channel (sponsorship value for the brand). The economics are partly captured in COGS (cans sold to Live Nation venues at wholesale), partly in sell-side activation budget (festival presence, on-stage placements). No clean way to disentangle this without inside data; folded into the standard breakdown without adjustment.

**The "consumer dollar anatomy" caveat applies as usual.** A consumer paying ~$2.50 for a single Liquid Death tallboy at 7-Eleven or ~$15 for an 8-pack at Target is paying for substantially more than just Liquid Death's cost structure: retailer markup (typically 30-50% gross on canned beverages), distributor margin where applicable, and sales tax. Liquid Death's revenue line is the wholesale price to the retailer/distributor, so the build_pct here is "Liquid Death's cost structure as a fraction of what Liquid Death earns from the retailer" — not "fraction of consumer dollar." Same caveat as Coca-Cola, Bud Light, and other beverage tickets.

**Why this matters as a chart point — the DTC/viral archetype.** Liquid Death is the deliberately-chosen example of a brand built primarily through marketing and content, where the product itself (canned water from a mountain) is functionally interchangeable with peers and the entire competitive moat is brand. At ~48% build_pct, it sits roughly 30 percentage points to the sell-heavy side of legacy beverage CPG. It's also a useful counterpoint to "category dictates ratio" — within the canned-beverage category, build_pct ranges from ~48% (Liquid Death) to ~67% (Coca-Cola) to ~82% (Bud Light), demonstrating that brand strategy and growth-stage choices drive enormous variance even within a single category. The chart should land this point clearly.

**Confidence: low — but the directional answer is robust.** Every single number in this analysis is an estimate, not a disclosure. Revenue is triangulated from press reporting; COGS is benchmarked from public peers and co-pack unit economics; marketing is anchored to founder interview commentary; sales-force, trade promotion, and overhead are typical-of-stage-and-channel guesses. The confidence rubric in plan.md reserves "low" for exactly this kind of private-company estimate and that's the honest call here. What gives me reasonable confidence in the *directional* result (sell-heavy outlier in the 40-55% build_pct range) is that the founder has been publicly consistent that marketing runs ~30%+ of revenue, and the implied build_pct only varies by ±5 points across a wide range of plausible cost-structure assumptions. The chart needs a "private DTC sell-heavy" data point and Liquid Death is the right one to use; readers should understand the bar carries ±5pp of uncertainty that the chart shouldn't pretend isn't there.
