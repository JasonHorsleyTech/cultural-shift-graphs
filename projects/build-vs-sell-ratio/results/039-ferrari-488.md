## Methodology notes

**Segment selection.** Ferrari N.V. reports three revenue line items in its 20-F: Cars and Spare Parts (€5,728M), Sponsorship/Commercial/Brand (€670M), and Other (€279M, which includes Ferrari Financial Services Inc., residual engine sales post-Maserati contract expiry, and Mugello racetrack). Per the ticket and plan.md:
- Excluded Sponsorship/Commercial/Brand entirely (F1 racing — see below).
- Excluded Ferrari Financial Services from "Other" per plan.md auto-financing carve-out. Financial Services revenue is bundled into the €279M Other line and not separately quantified in the press release; it is approximately €100–130M based on prior-year disclosures and the €1,662M receivables portfolio at year-end 2024.
- The Cars and Spare Parts segment accounts for 85.8% of total Ferrari revenue (€5,728M / €6,677M). Since Ferrari does not report segment-level P&L beyond revenues, all income statement cost items (COGS €3,329M, R&D €894M, SG&A €561M, EBIT €1,888M) are pro-rated at 85.8% to isolate the Cars segment. Pro-rata is highly defensible here given how concentrated Ferrari is: 85.8% of revenue is cars, and COGS is almost entirely automobile manufacturing, not F1 operations.

**F1 racing exclusion decision.** The Sponsorship/Commercial/Brand segment (€670M revenue in FY2024, up 17.1% driven by HP title sponsorship from 2024) is the F1 Scuderia Ferrari operation. Revenues include FIA prize money and commercial rights payments, title and associate sponsor fees (HP, Shell, Santander, etc.), and Ferrari brand licensing. This segment is NOT treated as "Ferrari's marketing spend" for the Cars segment ratio for two reasons: (1) it generates its own material revenue stream that partially or fully funds its costs, making it a distinct business rather than a pure cost center; (2) Ferrari's 20-F reports it as a separate segment with associated revenues, the standard signal that it has its own economics. Had it been counted as "Ferrari's advertising," sell_millions would increase by ~€400–500M (estimated net cost after sponsor revenue), which would mechanically lower build_pct to roughly 85–88% — still very high, but arguably overstates Ferrari's "selling effort" for cars since F1 is really a separate enterprise.

**Advertising: near zero once F1 is excluded.** Ferrari has essentially no traditional media advertising (no TV commercials, no outdoor, no print) for its car lineup. The company relies on: (a) Scuderia Ferrari F1 team presence as brand vehicle (excluded as above), (b) exclusive client event program (Cavalcade Classiche, Ferrari Challenge, XX Programs, Icone Stylist), (c) digital/social content, (d) auto show presence for new model debuts. There is no disclosed advertising line in Ferrari's 20-F — SG&A is reported as a single consolidated figure of €561M. External estimates (not from 20-F) put Ferrari's direct digital advertising around €40–50M/year globally, consistent with the overall 20% sell allocation used here.

**SG&A sell / overhead split.** The 40% sell / 60% overhead split applied to pro-rated SG&A ($521M) is the key estimation in this result. Ferrari's SG&A increased €92M year-over-year (FY23 ~€469M → FY24 €561M), with management citing "continuing initiatives for marketing and brand, as well as digital infrastructure and organizational development." The SG&A contains: client event hosting and travel, sales team compensation (Ferrari employs a global sales organization managing franchised and owned dealers), trade show costs, digital marketing, executive compensation, finance, legal, HR, and IT. A 40% sell attribution (~$209M) implies Ferrari spends roughly $15K per car delivered (13,752 units) on direct sales and marketing, which is plausible for a brand operating at €416K average revenue per car. Sensitivity: if sell attribution is raised to 50%, build_pct drops from 95.0% to 93.4%. Even at 60% sell attribution, build_pct = 91.8%. The ratio is highly insensitive to this split because sell is so small relative to build.

**R&D: 13.4% of revenue is the expensed P&L amount.** Ferrari's FY2024 R&D line is €894M (P&L expensed). Ferrari also capitalizes development costs: the FY2024 results presentation Industrial FCF bridge discloses approximately €476M in capitalized development costs during the year, amortized at approximately €331M (amortization of prior-period capitalized costs). The €331M amortization flows through the consolidated income statement (likely in COGS or a separate depreciation line), so it is captured within the pro-rated COGS already and is not double-counted here. R&D is all counted as build — Ferrari is developing new hybrid and fully-electric models (first full EV expected 2025), which are core engineering investment.

**Build_pct of 95.0% exceeds ticket prediction of 65–80%.** The ticket predicted 65–80%, noting Ferrari is "genuinely build-heavy." The data shows Ferrari is even more build-dominant than the prediction suggested. The gap is entirely explained by the sell side: Ferrari's explicit marketing costs are negligible once F1 is carved out, driving an exceptionally low sell denominator. This makes Ferrari the most build-concentrated entry in the consumer panel — a structurally appropriate result for a brand whose customer demand consistently exceeds supply and whose waiting lists obviate any need for demand-generation advertising. The 95% figure is not an artifact of methodology; it reflects reality.

**EUR → USD conversion.** All figures are converted from EUR using the 2024 calendar-year average exchange rate of 1.084 EUR/USD. The build_pct ratio is currency-neutral; the conversion affects only the absolute dollar amounts in the frontmatter.

**Confidence: high.** Ferrari is NYSE-listed (RACE) and files a full SEC 20-F with clean segment revenue disclosure, consolidated P&L with line-itemized COGS, R&D, and SG&A, and a detailed investor results presentation. The company is highly focused (one primary segment at 85.8% of revenue), making pro-rata cost allocation unusually reliable. The main estimation — SG&A sell/overhead split — has minimal impact on the ratio (±2pp over a wide range of split assumptions). The absence of a disclosed advertising line is not a data gap: Ferrari genuinely has near-zero traditional advertising, confirmed by qualitative disclosures in the 20-F and consistent with the brand's operating model.
