## Methodology notes

**Why the numbers are identical to ticket 040.** The rotisserie chicken is Costco's most famous product. It is not Costco's most separately-disclosed product. Costco files a single reportable segment (warehouse club operations worldwide) and does not itemize revenue, cost, or margin by SKU, category, or department in its 10-K. The rotisserie chicken's unit economics are openly discussed in investor communications and trade press, but those discussions surface qualitative descriptions ("we lose money to drive traffic") and occasional older loss estimates — not GAAP-audited financials. The only clean, audited cost structure available is consolidated Costco. That is what ticket 040 used, and what this ticket uses. The reported ratio of 98.3% is Costco-as-retailer, not rotisserie-chicken-as-SKU.

**The loss-leader unit economics (what we actually know).** The $4.99 price on the rotisserie chicken has not moved since 2009, through more than a decade of food inflation, two poultry-cost spikes, a pandemic, and multiple avian flu waves that hit commodity chicken prices hard. CFO Richard Galanti said on the Q2 FY2015 earnings call that Costco was "willing to eat $30–$40 million a year in gross margin" to hold the price when competitors (Sam's Club, most grocery chains) raised to $5.99. Industry analysis places the current all-in production cost per bird at $6–$7 (bird purchase price from contract farms, processing, labor, packaging, cooking gas/electricity), making the $4.99 retail price a loss of roughly $1–$3 per unit. At ~137M chickens sold in FY2023 (the last disclosed figure; estimated FY2024 volume is in that range or higher), the implied annual margin loss is $140–$400M — far larger than the 2015 disclosure. Costco has not provided an updated aggregate loss figure in recent 10-K filings or investor days.

**Lincoln Premium Poultry — vertical integration as a cost control measure.** In 2017, Costco broke ground on a company-owned poultry processing facility in Fremont, Nebraska (Lincoln Premium Poultry / LPP), opened September 2019 at a capital cost of approximately $450M. LPP operates a 400,000-sq-ft processing plant, a 90,000-sq-ft hatchery, and a 17,500-metric-ton feed mill, with ~100 contract-farming families across 17 Nebraska counties supplying ~2M birds per week. LPP covers an estimated 40% of Costco's US rotisserie chicken supply. The explicit goal was cost reduction: analysts and trade press estimate the LPP model saves approximately $0.35 per bird versus open-market procurement. At ~137M annual chickens, that is ~$48M per year in savings — meaningful but insufficient to return the product to profitability at $4.99. A Phase 2 expansion was shelved in late 2024 due to construction cost escalation. LPP's capital expenditure appears on Costco's consolidated capex line (not separately disclosed); the depreciation flows through COGS and is captured in the Build total above.

**Why we keep this as its own entry (vs. restructuring into the membership system).** Ticket 041 notes the structural parallel to the PlayStation system (ticket 020) and Nespresso (ticket 025), both of which were restructured to measure the full ecosystem rather than the loss-leader device. The rotisserie chicken was explicitly kept as a standalone entry by design: (a) the chicken is a complete, consumable product — not a platform — with no attached recurring-revenue SKU; (b) Costco's membership fee functions as the margin-recovery mechanism but it operates identically across every traffic-driver, not specifically through the chicken; (c) the chicken is iconic enough to deserve its own row precisely because the story IS the loss. The ratio at consolidated level (98.3%) is honest: Costco-as-company spends nearly everything on goods procurement and almost nothing on marketing. What that number cannot capture is the per-unit loss on this SKU specifically — and that gap is part of the story, not a data failure.

**Placement and behavioral economics — the strategy behind the loss.** Rotisserie chickens are placed at the rear of every Costco warehouse, adjacent to prepared foods, wines (typically 14%+ margin), and deli. The walk from entrance to chicken — through seasonal merchandise, roadshow tables, center-aisle impulse items — is structural, not accidental. Marketing literature (Quikly, Ernest Baskin at Saint Joseph's University) consistently notes that members who enter specifically for the $4.99 chicken add multiple items before reaching the rotisserie station. This means the $1–$3 per-unit chicken loss is partially recovered through higher-margin purchases in the same basket. The $4.99 price is also a membership-value signal: one of Costco's most-cited retention arguments is that you can buy a cooked 3-pound chicken cheaper than a raw uncooked chicken at most grocery stores.

**Back-of-envelope: what a chicken-level ratio might look like.** This is NOT in the frontmatter (which properly reports consolidated Costco), but for interpretive context: if one tried to compute a per-SKU ratio, the sell side of the rotisserie chicken would be approximately zero (no advertising, no trade promo, no slotting fees — it sells itself via myth and placement). The build side is production cost ($6–$7/bird) plus LPP capital amortization. Revenue is $4.99. Build_pct at SKU level would therefore exceed 100% — the chicken costs more to produce than Costco charges for it. This is the true answer to "what is the build vs. sell ratio for the Costco rotisserie chicken?" — it is a degenerate case where the product is not trying to cover its own costs.

**Confidence: medium.** Consolidated Costco financials are US-listed, audited, and directly reported — the income statement figures are high confidence. Confidence is capped at medium for the same reasons as ticket 040: COGS sub-allocation (merchandise vs. buying vs. occupancy) is estimated, not disclosed; and the consolidated result measures Costco-as-retailer, not rotisserie-chicken-as-product. The chicken's actual unit economics — which are the genuinely interesting question for this entry — are not disclosed in SEC filings and must be triangulated from earnings call color and industry analysis.
