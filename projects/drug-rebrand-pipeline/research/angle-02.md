# Angle 02 — Chemistry cutoff and candidate drug list

## The "is it really the same drug?" rubric

A core methodological question came up during workshopping: when can we say a rebranded medicine is "the same drug" as the street version, and when is the comparison too loose? We settled on a four-tier chemistry rubric, with one tier ruled out of scope.

### Tier 1 — Identical molecule

The medicine is literally the same chemical compound as the street drug. The only thing that changed is the name, the legal status, and (sometimes) the route of administration or formulation.

Examples:
- **Heroin ↔ Diamorphine.** Same molecule. Prescribed in the UK NHS for severe pain. The name change is purely jurisdictional and cultural.
- **Methamphetamine ↔ Desoxyn.** Desoxyn is methamphetamine hydrochloride, prescribed for ADHD and obesity. Same molecule, same enantiomer.
- **Cocaine ↔ Cocaine HCl (topical anesthetic).** Still used in ENT surgery. Same molecule, same name even.
- **GHB ↔ Sodium oxybate (Xyrem).** Different salt of the same active anion. Functionally identical.

### Tier 2 — Sibling molecule (same chemical class, similar mechanism)

The medicine and the street drug are not the same compound, but they're members of the same chemical family with overlapping pharmacology. Most people in the cultural conversation treat them as the same drug, and the rebrand mechanic is essentially the same.

Examples:
- **Methamphetamine ↔ Adderall.** Adderall is mixed amphetamine salts. Same neurotransmitter targets, similar effects, different molecule (no methyl on the nitrogen). Culturally "the same drug" for the rebrand argument; chemically a relative.
- **Ketamine ↔ Esketamine (Spravato).** Spravato is the S-enantiomer of ketamine. Same molecule minus one mirror-image half. Cleaner than sibling, sketchier than identical.

### Tier 3 — Cousin molecule (different scaffold, same receptor, same job)

The medicine and the street drug have different molecular structures but bind the same receptors to produce the same therapeutic effect. The substitution argument depends on mechanism, not chemistry.

Examples:
- **Heroin ↔ Methadone.** Methadone is a synthetic opioid with a totally different structure from heroin (not a morphinan). But it binds mu-opioid receptors to do the same job — opioid maintenance therapy.
- **Heroin ↔ Buprenorphine (Suboxone).** Partial mu-opioid agonist. Different scaffold, same receptor, same job.

### Tier 4 — Mechanism-only / vibes (CUT FROM PROJECT)

The two drugs produce vaguely similar feelings or touch the same broad neurotransmitter system, but the mechanism is materially different. These should NOT be ticketed.

Examples cut:
- "Caffeine is legal cocaine." Different receptors entirely (adenosine vs. dopamine reuptake). Cut.
- "SSRIs are legal MDMA." Both touch serotonin but the mechanisms are night and day. Cut.
- "Benzos are legal alcohol." Both GABA-ergic depressants but different binding sites. Borderline; cut anyway.

The cutoff rule: **same receptor + same therapeutic job = in. Just vibes = out.**

## Candidate drug list (developed live during workshopping)

What follows is the master list Jason and Claude generated top-of-dome. These become the wave 1 ticket list. Tiers reflect Jason's "how recognized is each name to the public" judgment.

### Tier A — most people recognize both names (highest priority)

1. **Heroin ↔ Methadone** (cousin) — substitution therapy classic
2. **Heroin ↔ Suboxone / Buprenorphine** (cousin) — partial agonist substitution
3. **Methamphetamine ↔ Adderall** (sibling) — the "culturally clean, chemically iffy" pair
4. **Marijuana ↔ Marinol / medical cannabis** (identical or sibling depending on form) — the medical-marijuana arc
5. **Ketamine ↔ Spravato (esketamine)** (sibling — single enantiomer) — depression treatment

### Tier B — meaningful chunk recognizes both names

6. **MDMA ↔ MDMA-assisted therapy** (identical; no consumer brand yet) — PTSD trials
7. **GHB ↔ Xyrem** (identical) — narcolepsy
8. **Opium / Morphine** — granddaddy of the pattern
9. **Cocaine ↔ topical anesthetic / Novocaine genealogy** — Novocaine is descended from but not the same as cocaine; topical cocaine HCl is identical
10. **Psilocybin ↔ psilocybin therapy** (identical) — treatment-resistant depression

### Tier C — niche, mostly known to people already reading about this stuff

11. **Methamphetamine ↔ Desoxyn** (identical) — chemically the cleanest case
12. **Heroin ↔ Diamorphine** (identical) — UK prescription opioid
13. **LSD ↔ historical Delysid / current trials** (identical)
14. **PCP ↔ Ketamine** (cousin via NMDA antagonism) — the surviving cousin
15. **Thalidomide ↔ Thalomid** (identical) — wildest arc

### Failed-rebrand null cases (important for honesty)

16. **Quaaludes / Methaqualone** — never made the rebrand jump
17. **PCP** — failed rebrand attempt
18. **Bath salts (synthetic cathinones)** — failed
19. **Crack cocaine** — no medical version
20. **DES (diethylstilbestrol)** — pulled, mostly

### Failed-medicine-rebranded examples (the second arc)

21. **Thalidomide → Thalomid** (identical molecule, new indication)
22. **Heroin / Bayer cough syrup → diamorphine** (covered above; spans both arcs)
23. **AZT** (cancer drug failure → first HIV drug)
24. **Sildenafil → Viagra → Revatio** (heart med flop → ED → PAH)
25. **Minoxidil → Rogaine** (BP med → hair growth)
26. **Bupropion (Wellbutrin) → Zyban** (depression → smoking)
27. **Finasteride (Proscar) → Propecia** (prostate → hair)
28. **Methotrexate** (chemo → low-dose RA / psoriasis)
29. **Fluoxetine (Prozac) → Sarafem** (depression → PMDD, evergreen)
30. **MDMA** (1970s therapy → club → therapy revival — spans both)
31. **LSD** (Delysid → club → trials — spans both)
32. **Lithium** (7-Up tonic → psychiatric medicine)

## Honesty checks to bake into research

- **Slow-release pharmacokinetics genuinely matter.** Don't let the rebrand framing dismiss real clinical differences. Adderall vs. meth experientially-and-clinically aren't interchangeable even when chemically close. Flag this in every relevant result.
- **"% of people who know the connection" is the squishiest data point in the project.** Wherever a real survey exists, cite it. Wherever it's triangulated, mark confidence.
- **Failed rebrands matter as much as successful ones.** The graph is more honest with Quaaludes and PCP in it than without them.
