# Drug rebrand pipeline

## The question

For every drug that has a recognized street-name identity AND a recognized medicine-name identity, how closely is the rebrand the *same drug* — and how aware is the public that the two are connected? How long did the rebrand take, and did it stick?

## The visceral hook

Adderall is slow-release Pervitin. Methadone is heroin maintenance with a respectable name. Spravato is ketamine in a nasal spray. Thalomid is the same molecule that caused thousands of babies to be born without limbs. People know the demon name. People know the medicine name. They mostly do not know they're the same — or close enough — drug.

This is "I wonder what that looks like on a graph" territory. Cultural amnesia, pharma rebrand strategy, and the question of how malleable a substance's reputation actually is.

## What we settled in workshopping

### Two related rebrand arcs (both in scope)

1. **Street drug → medicine.** Cultural demon gets a prescription identity. Heroin → methadone. Meth → Adderall. Ketamine → Spravato. MDMA → MDMA-assisted therapy.
2. **Failed/scandalous medicine → rebranded medicine.** Drug got pulled or shamed, came back under a different name for (often) a different indication. Thalidomide → Thalomid. Sildenafil → Viagra. Minoxidil → Rogaine. Fluoxetine → Sarafem. AZT.

A few drugs span both arcs (heroin was originally a Bayer cough syrup; MDMA was originally a psychotherapy adjunct; LSD was Sandoz's Delysid). These get full-arc tickets.

### Chemical-relationship rubric (the "is it the same drug?" cut)

Each rebrand pair gets classified on how close the chemistry actually is:

- **Identical molecule.** Heroin = diamorphine. Methamphetamine = Desoxyn. GHB ≈ sodium oxybate (Xyrem). The rebrand is purely cultural and legal.
- **Sibling molecule, same mechanism.** Adderall is amphetamine salts, not methamphetamine — chemically a relative, mechanistically similar. Spravato is the S-enantiomer of ketamine.
- **Cousin molecule, same receptor / same job.** Methadone and Suboxone are not heroin, but they hit the same opioid receptors to do the same job (opioid maintenance). Same with morphine/oxycodone in their internal genealogy.
- **Mechanism-only / vibes.** "Caffeine is legal cocaine," "SSRIs are legal MDMA." Cut from the project — too thin.

### Three metrics per drug

1. **Public awareness of the connection.** What % of people know the medicine and the demon are the same (or near-same) drug? Best-available proxies: surveys where they exist, news coverage of the connection, search-volume relationships, "did you know" content. Honest about uncertainty.
2. **Rebrand lag.** Years between "this drug is a scourge" (peak demonization) and "this drug is a medicine" (mainstream prescription / FDA approval / cultural normalization). Specific years where possible.
3. **Rebrand success over time.** Did it stick? Track current prescription volume, cultural perception, regulatory status. Note failed rebrands (Quaaludes, PCP, crack) as null cases — the graph is more honest with them than without them.

### Cut line

In scope: clear pair, same molecule or same mechanism doing the same job, both names culturally recognized by a meaningful chunk of people.

Out of scope (this project): drugs where the only link is "vaguely similar feeling" (caffeine/cocaine, SSRIs/MDMA, alcohol/benzos). Also out of scope: medicine→street arcs without a rebrand back (pure abuse stories — those are a different graph).

## Research honesty commitment

The framing ("it's the same drug, just renamed") presupposes that pharma is gaslighting the public. **The data should be allowed to contradict that.** Slow-release pharmacokinetics genuinely change a drug's abuse potential and clinical profile — Adderall and methamphetamine are not interchangeable in practice even when they're chemically close. If a rebrand is materially different from the demon version (different abuse profile, different efficacy, different safety), say so plainly in the result. The graph is more interesting if the spread is honest than if every pair is "yeah it's the same thing."

Public-awareness numbers especially should be honest: where survey data exists, cite it; where it doesn't, mark the figure as triangulated / estimated and give the reasoning.

## Two-wave research approach

- **Wave 1 (this batch of tickets):** every drug we could name during workshopping. Mix of clean cases, sketchier cases, null cases (failed rebrands). The point is breadth — see what the landscape looks like.
- **Wave 2 (planned, ~5 follow-up tickets per high-recognition drug):** after wave 1, review results and add follow-up tickets to deepen coverage of the drugs that scored highest on dual public recognition. Filter wave 2 tightly — only the strong both-side-recognized cases get expansion.

## Graph shape (working hypothesis — finalize during graph phase)

Most likely: a 2D scatter or small-multiple, with x = years from peak demonization to mainstream medical acceptance, y = % of public aware of the connection. Point size = current prescription volume or cultural footprint. Color or shape = chemical relationship (identical / sibling / cousin / mechanism-only).

Alternative: small-multiple timelines, one per drug, showing cultural status (street / illegal / medicine / mainstream medicine) over time. Both are viable — decide during the graph phase based on what the data looks like.

## Out of scope

- Medicine → street arcs without a rebrand-back component (OxyContin abuse, Adderall diversion in college). These are interesting but a separate project.
- International regulatory differences as the main story. Note where they matter (UK diamorphine, MDMA-AT trial geography) but don't structure the project around them.
- Recreational use rates as a primary metric. We're measuring rebrand success, not drug consumption.
