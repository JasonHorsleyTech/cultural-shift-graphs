# Ticket 024 — Lithium (7-Up tonic → psychiatric medicine)

## Demon identity

Lithium was never a "scary street drug." Its earlier identity is *as a soft-drink ingredient*: 7-Up, originally branded **Bib-Label Lithiated Lemon-Lime Soda** when launched by C.L. Grigg in 1929, contained lithium citrate as a mood-tonic ingredient. Lithium-containing tonics were popular cure-alls in the early 20th century (Lithia Springs Water, various patent medicines). Lithium was *also* used in salt substitutes for cardiac patients in the late 1940s, resulting in deaths from lithium toxicity and a brief FDA ban on lithium as a food/beverage additive (1949). 7-Up removed lithium from its formula in 1948.

## Medicine identity

**Lithium carbonate / lithium citrate** as a psychiatric medication, primarily for bipolar disorder. John Cade published the seminal paper in 1949 (Australia) showing lithium's antimanic effect. FDA approval for acute mania 1970, for maintenance 1974. Brand names: Eskalith, Lithobid, Lithonate, Lithotabs. Mostly prescribed generic today. WHO essential medicine.

## Arc type

`failed_medicine_rebranded`. The original "lithium as cure-all tonic" identity got pulled in 1948–1949 amid safety concerns. The same molecule was then rebranded ~20 years later as a *specific* psychiatric medicine with serum-level monitoring and a tight therapeutic index. Clean second-arc case.

## Chemistry hypothesis

**Identical.** Lithium is lithium — an element, not a synthesized compound. The salts vary (citrate in tonics, carbonate as the standard psychiatric prescription) but the active ion is Li+ in all cases.

## Caveats

- Lithium is a *naturally occurring element*, so it has cultural baggage in mineral-spring / spa traditions that pre-dates pharmaceutical use. Note in research but stay focused on the 7-Up-to-psychiatric arc.
- The "lithium in 7-Up" factoid is widely circulated online and serves as a major awareness signal. Public awareness of the connection is probably moderate-to-high among trivia-aware adults.
- Modern lithium prescribing has been declining in favor of newer mood stabilizers, even though efficacy data still supports lithium. Honesty section should reflect this — the rebrand worked but is being outcompeted by newer drugs.
