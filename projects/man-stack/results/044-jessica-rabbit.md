# Jessica Rabbit

## Character Summary
- **Source work:** Who Framed Roger Rabbit (1988), live-action / animated hybrid feature film directed by Robert Zemeckis, produced by Touchstone Pictures / Amblin Entertainment / Walt Disney Pictures, animation directed by Richard Williams. Based on Gary K. Wolf's 1981 novel *Who Censored Roger Rabbit?* (in which Jessica is portrayed as a manipulative gold-digger; the film's reimagining as morally good / loyal-to-husband is a substantial Zemeckis-era characterization shift). Speaking voice by Kathleen Turner (uncredited at the time of release); singing voice by Amy Irving. Jessica Rabbit is a Toon (cartoon character living in the live-action 1947 Los Angeles diegesis), works as a lounge singer at The Ink and Paint Club (a private club for Toons in the film's Hollywood-noir milieu), and is the wife of Roger Rabbit. Her introduction sequence (the curtain reveal performing "Why Don't You Do Right?") is one of the most-replayed pieces of male-target visual grammar in the history of feature animation.
- **Audience:** male-targeted
- **Decade slot:** 1980s
- **Cultural reach proxy:** Who Framed Roger Rabbit grossed approximately **$351M worldwide** on a ~$70M budget — the **second-highest-grossing film of 1988** behind Rain Man. Selected for preservation in the United States National Film Registry (Library of Congress, 2016) as "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant." The film is widely credited with kicking off the Disney Renaissance (The Little Mermaid 1989, Beauty and the Beast 1991, Aladdin 1992, The Lion King 1994) and revitalising adult-and-family animation generally (The Simpsons 1989, Ren & Stimpy 1991, Toy Story 1995). Jessica Rabbit specifically is one of the **most-recognized animated sex symbols of all time** — Empire ranked her sixth on a 2010 list of "best animated characters" *and* sixth on their list of greatest movie characters overall. The line "I'm not bad, I'm just drawn that way" was nominated for AFI's 100 Years…100 Movie Quotes shortlist and is a cultural touchstone for discussions of male-gaze cartoon representation. Cadbury Dairy Milk's 2014 industry survey ranked her the most alluring cartoon character ever (a marketing study, but symptomatic of the recurring placement). Continuous cosplay and merchandise presence from 1988 to present, with Jessica appearing in subsequent Disney parks media, the Roger Rabbit theme park area at Tokyo Disneyland, and recurring fan-art / pinup-tribute reproduction across pop culture for nearly forty years. The character is one of the platonic shorthand references for "animated character drawn explicitly as a male-fantasy object" in cultural criticism.
- **Canonical version used:** Per ticket instruction, the 1988 film. Jessica is portrayed as an adult woman of indeterminate-but-clearly-mature-twenties appearance; the 1947 Hollywood-noir diegesis treats her as a fully adult cabaret performer in her mid-to-late twenties. I treat her as ~25 for base-population purposes, sitting comfortably inside the project's standard 20–28 male-fantasy projection window. Cartoon-archetype mapping per ticket instruction: she has stylized, hyperbolic, intentionally-impossible body proportions (very long legs, dramatically narrow waist, very large bust, exaggerated red hair) — proportions that are openly acknowledged as impossible *within the film itself* via the meta-textual "drawn that way" line. Her body is to anatomy what Lisa from Weird Science (ticket 014) is to ontology — the male fantasy stack made literal, with the impossibility built into the conceit.

- **Currently single:** **NO — Jessica is canonically MARRIED to Roger Rabbit**, openly and devotedly so. - **Plays the protagonist's hobbies / aligned interest profile:** EXCLUDED. Following the convention set by Black Widow (ticket 010), Wanda Maximoff (ticket 012), and Lisa (ticket 014) — all excluded for non-specific niche-hobby alignment — this filter is excluded for Jessica.
- **High Sociosexual Orientation (top-quartile SOI-R):** EXCLUDED — close call, flagged. The whole engine of the film is the gap between her visual-promiscuity coding and her actual sexual-conservatism. Some asexual-icon readings (cited in cultural-criticism literature: CBR, QueerAF, The Statesman, Ace Couple podcast) explicitly argue Jessica is a *low-SOI* character whose appearance falsely codes high-SOI. The preferred reading (canonical-behavior-over-visual-coding) excludes the filter.
- **Doesn't follow mainstream "girl culture":** EXCLUDED. Jessica is the apotheosis of a particular flavor of conventional adult femininity (1940s noir cabaret-feminine: red sequined gown, long gloves, performed glamour, sultry vocal styling) — she is NOT alternative-coded, tomboy-coded, or Mean-Girls-anti-coded. The base-rate definition requires both (1) low engagement with mainstream celebrity / fashion culture AND (2) self-identification with an alternative interest profile (tomboy hobbies, anti-mainstream aesthetic). Jessica trivially clears (1) by virtue of living in 1947 Toontown rather than 2020s Instagram, but conspicuously fails (2). She is the platonic ideal of glamour-feminine performance, not an alternative-coded character. Same treatment as Princess Peach (ticket 028) and Yuna (ticket 018) — conventional-feminine archetypes do NOT get the not-girly filter even when set in pre-celebrity-media contexts. NOT counted.
- **Vulnerability / damsel:** Mixed — Jessica is threatened by Judge Doom and the weasels, briefly captured in the climax, and visibly afraid in some scenes; but she is also the *active rescuer* of Roger and Eddie at multiple points (saves Eddie from the Toon Patrol, fires a weapon at the climax, drives plot decisions). Per plan, vulnerability is flagged as personality and not quantified — excluded from calc, noted here.

## Personality / Characterization (Excluded from Calc)

- **The "drawn that way" meta-textual conceit:** Jessica's defining line — "I'm not bad, I'm just drawn that way" — is a *meta-textual acknowledgement that she is the male-fantasy stack made flesh*, with the male animators of the film as her authors. This is the same structural beat as Lisa from Weird Science (ticket 014, "constructed by protagonists / wish-fulfillment ontology"), but executed within the diegesis (Jessica acknowledges within the film that her appearance is a thing done TO her, not a thing she chose) rather than as the film's premise. For project purposes, Jessica is the *canonical animated example* of the meta-textual male-fantasy-construct character — comparable to Lisa as the canonical *live-action example*. The two characters share the same DNA: built explicitly to be the male fantasy, with the impossibility of the construction acknowledged within the work. The numerical joint probability (~7e-5 naive, ~1e-4 adjusted) understates the meta-textual fantastical level by orders of magnitude — same caveat as Lisa.
- **Loyal-to-husband-despite-appearance subversion:** The plot's central characterization beat is the gap between Jessica's femme-fatale visual coding and her actual moral-and-loyal canonical character. She chose Roger ("he makes me laugh") over more conventionally-acceptable partners; she stays with him through the film's threat-escalation; she risks herself to clear his name. This is one of the most-cited gender-and-genre subversion beats in late-1980s American cinema (the Wikipedia / cultural-criticism literature is consistent on this point). The "not bad, just drawn" line operates simultaneously as meta-textual acknowledgement AND as Jessica's plea to be seen as more than the visual she's been given. Personality / characterization beat — preferences split sharply on whether the "secretly-loyal-femme-fatale" framing is attractive or annoying, so excluded from the universal-desirable calc.
- **Lounge-singer / cabaret-performer occupational coding:** "Why Don't You Do Right?" performed at The Ink and Paint Club is the introductory and load-bearing visual sequence for the character. The cabaret-singer occupational frame is a 1940s-noir male-fantasy genre convention (compare Rita Hayworth in Gilda, 1946, the explicit visual reference per Richard Williams' design notes). Genre framing rather than a slider trait.
- **Femme-fatale visual archetype (later subverted):** Jessica's first 30 minutes of screen time are visually staged as the canonical noir femme-fatale (the curtain reveal, the spider-web introduction, the patty-cake-with-Acme reveal that frames the murder). The film then methodically subverts the archetype — but the archetypal coding is the visual surface that the male audience first encounters. Genre / staging, not a slider.
- **Hyperbolic cartoon proportions (the "drawn that way" anatomy):** Long red hair, dramatically narrow waist, very large bust, very long legs, tall stylized figure. Per ticket instruction, the cartoon-archetype mapping uses cultural projection (extreme buxom-with-impossibly-narrow-waist) rather than literal anthropometric measurement. Captured in the calc via the BMI+DD+ joint rate (0.02), with the caveat that her actual proportions sit BEYOND any human distribution.
- **Voice / vocal styling (Kathleen Turner speaking, Amy Irving singing):** Kathleen Turner's distinctively low/sultry speaking register and Amy Irving's chest-voice singing performance combine to give Jessica a vocal signature that is itself part of the male-fantasy projection. Some male audiences find sultry-low voices attractive; some don't. Preferences split → personality, not slider.
- **Married-but-acts-flirty (the patty-cake red herring):** The patty-cake sequence with Marvin Acme is initially staged as evidence of infidelity — and is then revealed to be literal (children's-game) patty-cake, a practical joke Acme insists on with all the women he meets. This characterization beat is structural to the plot's red-herring-and-reveal mystery rather than a universal-desirable slider.
- **Asexual icon dual-reading:** A meaningful share of the cultural-criticism literature reads Jessica as an *asexual icon*: a character whose visual sexualization is decoupled from any actual sexual interest in anyone other than her chosen partner. The reading is used in queer/asexual-community discussions of representation. Personality reading; preferences split sharply.
- **Toon-vs-human ontology:** Jessica is a Toon (an animated cartoon character existing in the film's mixed live-action/animation 1947 LA universe). The cross-ontology marriage to Roger Rabbit (a fellow Toon) is structurally the basis for her marital status. The Toon ontology is structural / world-building rather than a universal-desirable slider trait.

## Joint Probability

- **Method:** filter (male-target stack)
- **Naive product of marginals:**
  0.20 × 0.02 × 0.70 × 0.10 × 0.25
  = (0.20 × 0.02) = 0.004
  × 0.70 = 0.0028
  × 0.10 = 0.00028
  × 0.25 = **7.0e-5**
  (≈ 1 in 14,300, applied within a base population of US women aged 20–28)
  Note: the body-composition factor (0.02) is the BMI+DD+ JOINT rate, NOT the BMI marginal alone (0.22) — the 11x difference between marginal and joint is the dominant arithmetic move on Jessica's stack and reflects the rarity of the slim-waist + DD+ combination in real human anthropometry. ALSO note: the "currently single" (0.34) and "high-SOI" (0.25) filters are OMITTED because Jessica is canonically married-and-monogamously-devoted — both of those exclusions are arithmetic concessions to her canonical characterization. Including those two filters would push the joint toward ~6e-6 (~1 in 170,000) but would mis-represent the canonical character.
- **Correlation adjustment:** Mixed-direction adjustments. Pairs:
  - Top-quintile attractiveness ↔ BMI+DD+ joint: the joint rate (0.02) already encodes the body-composition rarity; the top-quintile attractiveness halo with BMI 18.5–22 specifically is mildly positive (slim figures rate higher in male-rated attractiveness studies; r ≈ 0.20–0.30) but the BMI+DD+ joint is a contradicting-traits combination that already accounts for this. Net effect on the (attractiveness × BMI+DD+) joint is approximately neutral — naive product is approximately correct here, with maybe 1.0–1.2x mild halo adjustment.
  - Top-quintile attractiveness ↔ low neuroticism: positive halo (r ≈ 0.10–0.15). Naive product OVERSTATES rarity by ~1.1–1.2x.
  - Top-quintile attractiveness ↔ Cool Girl Agreeableness: positive halo (r ≈ 0.10–0.15). OVERSTATES by ~1.1–1.2x.
  - Cool Girl Agreeableness ↔ low neuroticism: positive (both load on stability factor; r ≈ 0.20–0.25). OVERSTATES by ~1.2–1.3x.
  - **Net direction:** Three positive halos (attractiveness↔low-N, attractiveness↔Cool Girl, low-N↔Cool Girl) all in the rarity-overstating direction; one approximately-neutral pair (attractiveness↔body-joint). NO negative-correlation pair drives the adjustment toward "rarer than naive product" — Jessica's exclusion of the single filter and the not-girly filter means she AVOIDS the negative-correlation pairs (attractiveness↔single, attractiveness↔not-girly) that drive most male-target stacks toward "rarer than naive product." This is structurally similar to Princess Peach (ticket 028) — conventional-feminine characters who avoid the not-girly filter and who lack other negative-correlation pairs end up with positive-halo-dominant adjustments. Net adjustment: roughly **1.5–2x LESS rare than naive product**.
- **Adjusted estimate:** **~4.0e-5** (≈ 1 in 25,000 women aged 20–28). Bracket: [2e-5, 1e-4].
- **Population implication:** Out of ~14M US women aged 20–28, roughly **560** match Jessica Rabbit's universal-desirable stack as captured by the male-target methodology (range ~140–700 across the bracket). Jessica lands in the **mid-rare band** of the male-target distribution — comparable in numerical rarity to Lisa (ticket 014, ~280 matching) but pulled DOWN in calc-bound rarity by her exclusion of the single filter and the high-SOI filter (which Lisa's stack includes). She is meaningfully RARER than Princess Peach (ticket 028, ~2,100 matching) because the BMI+DD+ joint multiplier hits her at 0.02 versus Peach's marginal 0.22 (an 11x difference). She is meaningfully LESS rare than CJ Parker (ticket 016, ~15 matching) and 2B (ticket 032) because she lacks the additional skill-or-deep-filter multipliers those characters trigger. **The numerical answer is most informative when paired with the meta-textual point**: like Lisa, Jessica's measurable trait stack lands in the mid-rare band, but her *characterization* is the platonic male-fantasy stack made literal — she is the manifesto-character for the project hypothesis on the *animated* side, as Lisa is on the live-action side. The fact that even a deliberately-engineered "drawn-that-way" male-fantasy construct lands at "1 in 25,000" rather than "1 in a billion" is itself a finding: the universal-desirable trait list captures only a portion of what makes Jessica Jessica. The real fantasy-stack for this character is in the *characterization* (drawn-that-way meta-textuality, lounge-singer iconography, secretly-loyal subversion) — which the calc explicitly excludes. This is consistent with the project's recurring finding (Princess Leia, Rachel Green, Lisa, Princess Peach) that male-target heartthrobs derive much of their cultural weight from CHARACTERIZATION and ICONIC IMAGERY rather than from maxing universal sliders.

- **Trait identification:** high — Jessica's traits in the 1988 film are exhaustively documented (Wikipedia, Disney Wiki / Fandom, TV Tropes, cultural-criticism retrospectives, Richard Williams' published design statements, Empire / AFI rankings, Kathleen Turner interviews, decades of academic and popular treatments of the male-gaze in animation). - **Correlation adjustments:** medium — directional claim that the positive halos dominate (because Jessica's stack avoids the negative-correlation pairs that drive most male-target adjustments toward rarer-than-naive) is well-supported in mate-market and Big-5 literature, but the exact magnitude (1.5–2x net less rare) is judgment, not a measured joint distribution. Bracketing of [2e-5, 1e-4] is wide for this reason.
- **Cartoon-archetype mapping caveat:** medium-low confidence reduction. ## Notes / Edge Cases

- **The animated counterpart to Lisa.** Jessica Rabbit and Lisa from Weird Science (ticket 014) are structurally the same character at different ontological registers: both are *deliberately constructed male fantasies whose construction is acknowledged within their respective works*. Lisa is Frankenstein-by-Barbie-and-Einstein in live action; Jessica is "drawn that way" in feature animation. Both bear the meta-textual signature of "the male-fantasy stack made flesh, with the male authors of the work openly inscribing themselves as Pygmalion." Both land in the mid-rare band of the male-target distribution arithmetically (Lisa ~280 matching, Jessica ~560 matching) — meaningfully rarer than naturalistic-iconic characters (Rachel Green, Linda Barrett, Daisy Duke) but not in the deep-rare extreme-stack band (CJ Parker, Mikaela, Black Widow, Wonder Woman). The numerical answer for both characters understates the meta-textual fantasy level by orders of magnitude because the universal-desirable trait list cannot capture "the work itself acknowledges this woman is impossible." For the final graph and write-up, Jessica should be flagged alongside Lisa as the project's two manifesto-characters — the explicit meta-textual reference points for the male-fantasy stack hypothesis. Lisa is the live-action manifesto; Jessica is the animated manifesto. (Compare to Christian Grey, ticket 009, who is the manifesto character on the female-target side — but Christian's text treats him as a real-if-extremely-improbable human, whereas Lisa and Jessica explicitly acknowledge the impossibility within their diegesis.)
- **Beyond-the-distribution body proportions.** Jessica's actual drawn proportions (waist-to-bust ratio, leg-to-torso ratio, head-to-body ratio) sit OUTSIDE any plausible human anatomical distribution. The methodology uses the BMI+DD+ joint rate (0.02) as the closest defensible cap on rarity — but a strict application of "no real human matches" would give a rate of approximately zero. The methodology choice here is to treat her as if she were drawn at the rarest plausible-human extreme (slim-waist + natural-DD+) rather than as an anatomical impossibility, which is the same call made for Princess Peach, Yuna, and other cartoon characters. A reviewer who insisted on penalizing the impossibility further would push Jessica's joint to ~1e-7 or smaller, which would put her in the deepest rare band of the dataset alongside Christian Grey. The chosen methodology preserves comparability across cartoon and live-action characters at the cost of some understatement of the cartoon-impossibility.
- **Why "currently single" is excluded — and why this is the most interesting arithmetic fact about Jessica's stack.** Most male-target heartthrobs trivially clear the single filter (Princess Leia is the closest comparable case where she is paired across the canonical trilogy). Jessica is one of the most-iconic male-fantasy figures in the dataset, AND she is canonically married. The arithmetic concession (omit the single filter, ~3x less rare as a result) is an honest concession to her characterization. The cultural fact that Jessica functions as a male-fantasy projection *despite* canonical marriage is itself interesting — it suggests male-fantasy projection can survive (or even be enhanced by) canonical attached-status when the character is visually-coded for projection. The "drawn that way" line works on this register too: Jessica is acknowledging that she has been drawn for a male audience even though her canonical interest is in her husband. The asexual-icon reading in the cultural-criticism literature is downstream of this same observation.
- **High-SOI exclusion was a close call.** Jessica's visual coding is unambiguously high-SOI (the curtain-reveal introduction, the slit-dress, the patty-cake euphemism, the cabaret-singer occupational frame). Her canonical behavior is unambiguously monogamous-loyal. The character is a designed-in mismatch between visual coding and canonical sexuality.
