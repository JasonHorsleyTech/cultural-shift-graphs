# Loss-leader economics

## The idea

Many famous "products" are intentionally sold at a loss to lock you into a higher-margin consumable or service. Map the depth of the cross-subsidy across a bunch of them.

Candidates:
- Gillette razor (cheap handle, expensive blades)
- Nespresso machine (cheap machine, expensive pods)
- PS5 (sold at/below cost; Sony makes money on game licensing, accessories, PS Plus)
- Nintendo Switch (less subsidized than PS5, but heavy reliance on first-party game sales)
- Peloton bike (near-cost hardware, $40/mo membership is the actual product)
- Keurig (same as Nespresso)
- Kindle (Amazon ebooks)
- HP printers (cheap printer, ink cartridges are obscene)
- Cell phone carrier subsidies historically (free phone, locked into 2-year contract)

## The graph

For each product, two bars side by side: device margin (often near zero or negative) vs. consumable/service margin per unit lifetime. Or one bar showing "lifetime spend on the product family vs. sticker price of the device."

Either visual makes the cross-subsidy visceral: you didn't buy a Nespresso machine, you signed up for a coffee subscription with a hardware down-payment.

## Why it's interesting

Surfaces a business model most people only vaguely know about. The numbers are likely shocking — e.g. lifetime spend on Nespresso pods can be 10x+ the machine price. Pairs naturally with the build-vs-sell project (which had to restructure these products because the SKU-level numbers were misleading).
