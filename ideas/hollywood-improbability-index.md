# Hollywood Improbability Index

## Core hypothesis

Movies have gotten more and more unrealistic. What used to be a realistic genre has turned into something fantastical. We can measure this objectively — every protagonist's situation has a probability, and that probability is collapsing over time.

## The Simpsons example

Homer:
- One job, not very skilled, basically a desk job (joke job, but canonically real in-show)
- Owns two cars
- 3BR / 2BA house in the suburbs
- Single income
- Considered *poor* by the show's framing

In the 1980s this was actually plausible — that's what "poor with a family" looked like on a single income. In 2026 it's fantastical. The show didn't change. Reality moved underneath it.

## Two genres, same fantasy

The pitch: action movies and romance/drama are doing the same thing — empowerment fantasy via implausibility. We just don't think of romance that way.

### Action side (male-coded empowerment)

- **Hawkeye in Avengers** jumps onto a flying alien skateboard and hits a moving target at ~60mph. Not humanly possible.
- **John Wick** is the cleanest case. To be John Wick you'd need to be in the top 1% of shooting skill × top 1% of hand-to-hand × top 1% of driving × top 1% of hacking (these are unrelated skills, so they multiply). Plus luck. You're looking at 1-in-millions to land the actual outcome of the movie.
- Men watch this and (some fraction of them) believe they could beat a gorilla in a fight. Combat skill is wildly overestimated by people who have never been in a fight.

### Romance side (often female-coded empowerment, but the framing shouldn't be gendered)

- **Pride and Prejudice**: lady gets the most eligible bachelor in the world. Improbable.
- The modern wedding movie: couple in their 20s, Italian wedding, perfect white bridesmaid dresses, $150k production. What % of 20-something American couples can afford that? ~1%? Less?
- This is just as much an empowerment fantasy as John Wick. The math is the same — multiply the probabilities of each component (right age × right income × right partner × right aesthetic outcome) and you get a tiny number.

The key reframe: **don't cut this up by gender.** It's all empowerment fantasy. Raw probability is the unit.

## The audience-side flip (added later)

The fantasy works because audiences misjudge the underlying probabilities. Symmetric blindness:

- **18-year-olds** hilariously overestimate how much money they'll make after college.
- **20-year-olds** hilariously overestimate how much rent they can afford — they look at the Friends apartment and don't register that it's a top-1% Manhattan unit.
- **Women** watching wedding scenes don't go "oh shit, that's $150k" — they go "Italy looks nice, maybe that's my preference." They're aesthetically aware, financially blind.
- **Men** watching John Wick don't go "oh shit, that's 10,000 hours of training on a Glock under stress" — they go "I'd probably do alright in a gunfight." Aesthetically aware, skills-blind.
- **20-year-olds watching Homer Simpson** being asked "do you want kids?" hilariously underestimate the cost of providing for a family of five, two cars, suburban house, single income, dinner at home — because The Simpsons told them that's the floor of what life looks like.

Same cognitive failure mode, different domains. The empowerment fantasy is asymmetric: the audience absorbs the lifestyle as default-achievable while the actual probability of pulling it off is ~1%.

## What the graph could look like

For every major movie of the last 50 years, score the protagonist's situation on probability of replication. Multiply the component probabilities (skills, wealth, partner, lifestyle outcomes). Plot the median improbability score by year, by genre. Hypothesis: every genre's improbability has gone up. Romance and drama have caught up with action.

Bonus cuts:
- Improbability split by genre (action / romance / drama / sitcom / coming-of-age)
- Improbability of *lifestyle* (Homer's house, Friends apartment, Sex and the City closet) vs improbability of *plot* (John Wick's body count, Pride and Prejudice's match)
- Audience perception gap: ask people "how realistic is this?" and compare to the actual probability score

## Open questions / things to sharpen later

- How do you score "improbability" consistently across an action plot and a romance plot? Need a unified rubric. Maybe: P(component) per component, multiplied, with components defined as "named lifestyle/outcome facts a viewer would notice."
- Is the decline real or are we just better at noticing it now? Need pre-1980 baseline.
- The Simpsons isn't getting more fantastical — *reality* is moving away from it. That's a different mechanism than John Wick (which got more fantastical on its own). Worth separating: "movies escalated" vs "reality regressed."
- Where do sci-fi and fantasy fit? They've always been improbable on purpose. Maybe exclude, or score only the protagonist's *non-genre* attributes (relationships, money, lifestyle).
- Is there a "Goldilocks zone" of believable? Probably need a cutoff like "improbability < 1-in-100 = grounded, 1-in-10k = aspirational, 1-in-1M = fantasy."
