# Ticket 026 — Fentanyl

## Demon identity

**Fentanyl** (and its analogs — carfentanil, sufentanil, alfentanil, and illicit analogs like furanyl-fentanyl). Cultural demonization arrived late but intensely — from ~2014 onward as illicit fentanyl (mostly produced in clandestine labs, originally trafficked through China and now primarily through Mexico) began contaminating the heroin and counterfeit-pill supply. Now the leading cause of overdose death in the US.

## Medicine identity

**Fentanyl** is also a long-established FDA-approved prescription opioid. Janssen developed it (Paul Janssen, 1959). FDA approved as Sublimaze (IV anesthesia) 1968. Duragesic transdermal patch 1990. Actiq lollipop (breakthrough cancer pain) 1998. Sublingual sprays, lozenges, sublingual tablets. Used routinely in surgical anesthesia, palliative care, ICU. Schedule II.

## Arc type

`both` in unusual configuration: this is *medicine first* (1968), then street-drug demonization much later (2014+), and the medicine identity remains active throughout. The street/demon identity emerged 50 years after the medicine, which is unusual in the project.

## Chemistry hypothesis

**Identical** for prescription fentanyl vs. street fentanyl (when illicit fentanyl is pure fentanyl). However, street "fentanyl" frequently contains analogs (carfentanil, valeryl-fentanyl, etc.) which are siblings/cousins. Honesty section should reflect this — street fentanyl is rarely as simple as "pharmacy fentanyl that escaped."

## Source hierarchy

1. **FDA Sublimaze approval (1968), subsequent fentanyl product approvals.**
2. **Janssen historical materials** on Paul Janssen's discovery.
3. **CDC overdose mortality data** — fentanyl-involved deaths by year.
4. **DEA fentanyl analog scheduling** — emergency scheduling actions.
5. **DEA ARCOS data** for prescription fentanyl distribution.
6. **Anesthesiology / palliative care guidelines** for current legitimate use.

## Caveats

- Like ticket 025 (OxyContin), this is an edge case for the project. The arc is medicine → street, not the typical street → medicine direction. Include because Jason wanted breadth.
- The street fentanyl crisis is recent and ongoing — current-status reporting should be honest about the unfinished arc.
- Public awareness that "fentanyl is also a normal hospital anesthetic" is probably low. Most Americans now associate fentanyl exclusively with overdose deaths.
- The rebrand-success metric is awkward here — fentanyl wasn't rebranded, the *same* prescription product continued unchanged while a parallel street identity emerged.
