# Edward Lewis

## Character Summary
- **Source work:** *Pretty Woman* (1990, film), Touchstone Pictures, dir. Garry Marshall, written by J. F. Lawton (original screenplay titled *3000*). Edward Lewis portrayed by Richard Gere; Vivian Ward by Julia Roberts.
- **Audience:** female-targeted
- **Decade slot:** 1990s
- **Cultural reach proxy:** Worldwide gross ~$463.4M on a ~$14M budget (one of the most profitable films of its era and the fifth-highest-grossing film of 1990). Approximately 42.2M tickets sold in the US — the highest ticket count for any romantic comedy on record. Routinely ranked on AFI / BBC / Empire / Cosmopolitan / Glamour "best romantic-comedy" and "best movie boyfriend" retrospectives 35 years later. Defining cultural reference for the "Cinderella + corporate raider" / "silver fox billionaire" archetype that later commercial romance fiction (50 Shades, etc.) explicitly borrows from.
- **Canonical version used:** 1990 theatrical film. The 2018 stage musical *Pretty Woman: The Musical* substantially preserves the Edward character but is not used as canon here; the film is the dominant cultural artifact.

Traits considered and excluded:

- **Height (≥ 6'2")**: Richard Gere is canonically 5'10" (177.8 cm). The character is not depicted or described as notably tall — the framing in the film is "elegant, well-tailored older man," not "towering." Does NOT clear ≥ 6'2". Excluded.
- **Physical fitness (top quintile)**: Edward is depicted as lean and well-groomed but not muscular or athletic. No on-screen training, sport, or fitness behavior. He is afraid of heights (canon: refuses to step onto the penthouse balcony at first). At ~40 years old in 1990 with a sedentary high-finance lifestyle, the strict "visible muscle, athletic, top-quintile NHANES body composition" reading does NOT apply. Excluded.
- **Musical/artistic skill (professional/virtuoso)**: The famous hotel-ballroom piano scene is iconic — Gere actually improvised the piece on set, and the scene cemented "Edward plays piano" as part of the character. However, in canon Edward is shown playing alone in the middle of the night as a private hobby/release, not as a trained concert pianist. The performance is one moment, not a sustained portrayal of mastery. Reading him as a "skilled hobbyist pianist" rather than career-tier virtuoso is the honest call; that tier is too common (~1–5% of US adults) to register as a slider-maxed trait at the project's expert thresholds. Excluded from the joint, noted in Edge Cases.
- **Additional expert skills (chef / pilot / surgeon / mechanic / etc.)**: None established. His business expertise is his profession, not a stacked side skill — by project convention, the protagonist's job doesn't count as an "additional expert skill" trait. Excluded.
- **Languages beyond English**: No canonical evidence Edward speaks any language other than English. He attends opera (*La Traviata* in San Francisco) and demonstrates familiarity with classical-music culture, but does not speak Italian, French, or other languages on screen. Excluded.
- **Age in prime window (28–38)**: Richard Gere was 40 during filming; the character is depicted as a mid-career corporate raider plausibly in his early 40s. Edward is canonically *outside* the 28–38 prime window — he is the "silver fox / older-but-distinguished" archetype, which is exactly what the ticket notes predicted. Trait does NOT apply. Excluded. See Notes for the silver-fox sub-archetype framing.

## Personality / Characterization (Excluded from Calc)

- **Cold corporate-raider / "buys companies and breaks them up" coded ruthlessness**: Defining personality archetype; preferences split — some readers find it sexy ("dominant alpha"), some find it repulsive (Gordon Gekko-adjacent). Polarizing.
- **Tortured / abandoned by father, traumatic childhood**: The "fix-the-broken-billionaire" caretaker hook the romance genre relies on. Per plan, "mysterious dark past" is personality-coded and excluded from the calc.
- **Emotionally reserved, "doesn't drink alcohol," doesn't take phone calls in his ear, afraid of heights, doesn't drive**: The "vulnerability beneath the armor" archetype. Personality, not slider.
- **Pygmalion / "makeover the working girl into a lady" arc**: Plot device drawing on classical Pygmalion / *My Fair Lady* template; not a universal-desirable trait. Polarizing in modern reception (some readers love it, some critique it as condescending and class-coded).
- **Gentleness with Vivian / pulls back from sexual aggression / treats her with respect**: Personality coding, not slider.
- **Connoisseur of opera, classical piano, fine dining, polo, fashion**: Cultural-capital characterization. Some readers find it attractive, some find it alienating bourgeois posturing. Preferences split.
- **Career change at the end (decides to invest in rather than break up Morse Industries)**: Character-arc resolution, not a stacked desirable trait.
- **"Silver fox" older-but-distinguished sex appeal**: Tonal/aesthetic personality, NOT a universal-desirable in the female-target threshold list (which uses the 28–38 prime window as the universal slider). The silver-fox appeal is real but operates via a different demographic preference channel. Noted but not counted.

## Joint Probability

- **Method:** threshold (female-target stack)
- **Naive product of marginals:**
  3e-6 × 0.02 × 0.05
  = 3e-6 × 0.02 = 6e-8
  × 0.05 = **3e-9**
  (≈ 1 in 333 million)
- **Correlation adjustment:**
  - **Wealth ↔ intelligence (positive halo, pulls less rare).** Building a multibillion-dollar LBO firm is positively correlated with above-average cognitive ability — IQ-wealth correlations within the high-cognitive-occupations population are well-established (r ≈ 0.20–0.30 between cognitive ability and earnings at the upper tail per Strenze 2007 meta-analysis). Per base-rates.md: "negatively correlated with billionaire-tier wealth via time-budget at extreme tails — but mildly positive at the moderate tail." For Edward's stack (just three traits, no time-budget skill stacking), the relevant joint is the moderate-tail positive halo. Naive product overstates rarity for the wealth × IQ joint by ~2–3x. Pulls joint **~2–3x less rare**.
  - **Wealth ↔ family unencumbered (approximately independent).** No strong joint signal. Divorced status among ultra-wealthy men is *more* common than the population average (high-stress careers + serial-relationship base rates), but the difference is modest. Approximately independent at the joint level. No adjustment.
  - **Older age bracket reduces the conditional rarity of billionaire status (pulls less rare).** Edward is ~40, NOT in the 28–38 prime window. Per base-rates.md: "Strongly positively correlated with age — median US billionaire is 67. Conditional rate among adults aged 28–38 is roughly 5–10x lower than the marginal." The flip side: at age 40+, the conditional billionaire rate is *higher* than the marginal rate of 3e-6. Adult men 40+ have roughly 2–3x the marginal billionaire rate (most billionaires are 50+, but the 40s are when self-made wealth tends to crystallize). Pulls joint **~2–3x less rare** if conditioning on age — but since age 28–38 is NOT a stacked trait here, this adjustment is implicit, not double-counted.
  - **Net direction:** The total adjustment is mild and runs in the "less rare" direction. Edward Lewis's stack is structurally short (3 universal-desirable traits) and lacks the time-budget-binding mastery stack (pilot+chef+pianist+kickboxer+polyglot+billionaire-by-27) that drives Christian Grey and Edward Cullen into the 1e-20s. With no skill-stacking, no time-budget conflicts, and only mild positive correlations among the three traits, the net adjustment is roughly **~3–5x less rare than naive** — small in absolute terms.
- **Adjusted estimate:** ~**1e-8** (≈ 1 in 100 million). Bracket: [3e-9, 3e-8].
- **Population implication:** Out of ~127M US adult men (Census 2024), expected matches ≈ **1.3** — roughly *one* US adult man matches Edward Lewis's full universal-desirable stack at the threshold definitions used here. Out of ~3.5B global adult men, expected matches ≈ ~35. This is **orders of magnitude more achievable than Christian Grey** (~2e-24, fewer than one human in all of human history) or Edward Cullen (~1e-18). Edward Lewis is squarely in the "this person actually exists" tier of the female-target distribution — his appeal is dominated by archetype, age-tier, and personality (the silver-fox vulnerable-billionaire), not by slider-maxing. He sits considerably rarer than personality-archetype heartthrobs like Jack Dawson (~1.5e-5) and Han Solo (similar tier) primarily because the billionaire-tier wealth threshold alone supplies most of the rarity (3e-6 dominates the product), not because of stacked skill mastery.

## Confidence Assessment
- **Overall:** medium
- **Trait identification:** high — Edward's traits are clearly established in the film (corporate raider, divorced, estranged father deceased, ex-girlfriend, no kids, sedentary high-finance lifestyle, mid-40s). The stack is short and unambiguous.
- **Base rates:** medium — billionaire base rate (3e-6) and IQ-130 base rate (0.02) are both well-grounded in their entries. Family-unencumbered (0.05) is a reasoned composite. The dominant calibration risk is the wealth-tier choice (see Notes).
- **Correlation adjustments:** medium-high — three traits with mostly mild positive correlations and no time-budget conflicts. Direction and magnitude are well-supported and the net adjustment is small.

## Notes / Edge Cases

- **The silver-fox sub-archetype.** Edward Lewis is the project's clearest example of a *distinct sub-archetype* on the female-target side: the "older-but-distinguished" wealthy man, distinguished from the "young billionaire-pianist-pilot" (Christian Grey, Edward Cullen) and "working-class artist-savior" (Jack Dawson) modes. The silver-fox archetype trades skill-stacking and youthfulness for age, social capital, and the specific appeal of being "settled, accomplished, vulnerable beneath the armor." When the final distribution is plotted, Edward should sit at a deliberately different position than the slider-maxed billionaires — he's rare via wealth tier and family status, not via stacked mastery. This is real signal about how the female-target heartthrob distribution is *structured*: it has multiple sub-modes, and the silver-fox mode lives at a moderate-rarity position, not the extreme tail.
- **Wealth-tier calibration is the dominant risk.** The film does not quote a specific net worth for Edward. The ticket explicitly directs to map to billionaire-tier (3e-6), which I followed. Justification: in 1990, the on-screen depiction of Edward's operations (hostile takeovers of major industrial concerns, helicopter/jet, polo-circuit social tier, Beverly Wilshire penthouse for extended residence, $1B-class deal sizes implied by the Morse Industries arc) maps onto the actual top-tier 1990 LBO operator class — Kravis, Icahn, Pickens, Milken — all of whom were billionaires by 1990. A more conservative reading ("very wealthy mid-tier LBO partner, $50M–$500M net worth") would use the $10M+/yr income marginal (2e-4) instead, shifting the joint by ~70x toward less rare (joint ≈ ~7e-7, ≈ 1 in 1.5M). This calibration choice is the single largest swing factor in the headline number; the bracket [3e-9, 3e-8] reflects the within-billionaire-tier uncertainty, NOT the cross-tier uncertainty. A reviewer adopting the sub-billionaire reading would land Edward at ~1e-6 to 1e-7, which would still place him in the "rarer than personality-archetype heartthrobs but much less rare than Christian Grey" zone — the qualitative finding holds either way.
- **Age outside prime window — methodological consistency.** The plan's prime-window threshold (28–38) is calibrated to the typical "powerful but still hot" depiction common in romance leads. Edward Lewis at ~40 is the project's first character explicitly *outside* the prime window on the older end (Jack Dawson and Johnny Castle were below it on the younger end). This is exactly the silver-fox case the ticket flagged. Following the methodology faithfully, the trait does NOT apply, and not counting it is the right call — but it's worth noting in the final analysis that the 28–38 window misses both the youthful-charmer mode (Jack at 20, Johnny at 25) AND the silver-fox mode (Edward at 40+). If the project wanted to replace this binary trait with a continuous "age desirability curve," Edward would still be inside a desirable bracket (just a different one). Don't reweight retrospectively, but flag the project-level finding: the prime-window threshold is *narrower* than the actual female-target preference distribution, and the threshold methodology slightly overstates rarity for characters at the older or younger tails of that distribution.
- **Piano scene — calibration note.** The hotel-ballroom piano scene is one of the most iconic moments in the film, and there is a tempting reviewer impulse to count it as a stacked virtuoso trait (1e-4). Honest reading: Gere improvised the piece on set, and in canon the scene shows Edward playing alone in the middle of the night as a private outlet, not performing publicly or trained at concert level. The piano isn't stacked as part of his identity in the way Christian Grey's nightly Bach/Chopin practice is. Counting it would push the joint to ~1e-12, dramatically overstating his rarity and inflating his profile beyond what the canon supports. Excluded from the calc; flagged here for transparency. A reviewer who reads Gere's scene as "trained classical pianist tier" rather than "skilled hobbyist who can improvise pleasantly" would push the joint by ~1e4 toward more rare.
- **Personality dominance flag.** Like Jack Dawson and Han Solo, Edward Lewis's enduring rom-com canon status is more about archetype than about stack rarity. The "vulnerable corporate raider redeemed by sex worker with a heart of gold" arc is iconic Pygmalion-genre romance — and the *Pretty Woman* franchise's cultural footprint (1990 highest-ticket rom-com ever, modern-day "fairy tale" reference, stage-musical adaptation) is far larger than a 1-in-100M trait stack would explain on its own. Edward's appeal is concentrated in personality (cold-billionaire-melted-by-warmth) and tonal positioning (silver-fox, sophisticated, vulnerable beneath the armor) more than in slider-stacking. This is the same partitioning observed in Jack Dawson and supports the project's emerging finding that the female-target heartthrob distribution likely partitions cleanly into "stack-rarity types" (Christian Grey, Edward Cullen) and "personality/archetype types" (Jack Dawson, Han Solo, Edward Lewis).
- **Compared to other female-targeted leads processed so far.** Adjusted joint probability (~1e-8) places Edward Lewis between the personality-dominant Jack Dawson tier (~1.5e-5) and the slider-maxed Christian Grey tier (~2e-24). His position is driven almost entirely by the billionaire-tier wealth marginal (3e-6, which dominates the product), with intelligence and family-unencumbered status contributing modestly. He stacks ZERO mastery skill traits (no pilot, chef, virtuoso music, languages, or fitness). This makes Edward a useful within-distribution test case: the silver-fox archetype lands at moderate rarity *despite* being a "billionaire," because the canonical version of the character is short on stacked skills. The stack-rarity mode of female-target heartthrobs requires *both* extreme wealth AND skill-stacking; one without the other lands at very different rarity tiers.
- **Cultural footprint vs. stack rarity asymmetry.** *Pretty Woman* (1990) sold the most rom-com tickets in US history, and Edward Lewis is one of the most reproduced "wealthy man romance" templates in subsequent fiction. Yet his trait stack is among the *least* improbable on the project's female-target side. This is direct quantitative evidence that the cultural-staying-power of a heartthrob can be substantial without slider-maxing — archetype, mode, and personality carry their own enormous weight. The graph annotations should make clear that "rarer ≠ more popular" — if the male-target distribution turns out to land in similar moderate-rarity zones with a different mode of stacking, the comparison is fundamentally about *which slider* the genre maxes (achievement vs. perfection-and-frictionlessness), not which is more popular or more unrealistic.
